# Combe Downer

A multiplayer first-person shooter in the browser, set in Bath. Each map is a circle inside a force
field, built from real open data: Overture Maps buildings, roads, walls, woods and water, on
Environment Agency lidar terrain.

- **Combe Down**: the village around the Hadley Arms, inside the whole of Bath: the force field
  is 11 km across, from Lansdown and the racecourse down to Wellow, and from Newton Park to
  Bathford. The city is built to the same detail as the village (gardens, walls, doors, lamps,
  parked cars, shop signs), in squares about a kilometre across that load
  in around you as you go, and it all plays the same everywhere: traffic, naughty boys, cats, pickups,
  buses to call, cars to drive and balls on the pitches, in town just as in the village. Grab a car
  and drive into town. Up on Bathampton Down stands Eric's
  oak, in memory of our friend Eric Benjamin Holmes. Down in Twerton is Twerton Park, Bath City's
  ground: the old Main Stand with BATH CITY picked out in its seats, the covered Popular Side, the
  big open Bristol End, the shallow Bath End, the dugouts, hoardings and floodlight pylons. You can
  climb the terraces and sit in the stand.
  In the middle of town, three of Bath's great buildings are modelled by hand:
    - **Bath Abbey**: the angels climbing their ladders up the west front, the great west and east
      windows, flying buttresses and pinnacles, and the tower over the crossing. Go in through the
      west door to the nave under its fan vault, with the stained glass, pews, choir and organ.
    - **Pulteney Bridge**: three arches over the Avon, Adam's front with its Venetian window and
      domed pavilions, the odd backs of the shops upstream, and a shopfront each side of the street
      with the names of the shops there now. Pulteney Weir's horseshoe of white water is just below.
    - **The Roman Baths**: go in from the Abbey Churchyard through the Concert Room, under its glass
      dome, onto the terrace with its statues of emperors. Take the stairs down to the steaming
      green water of the Great Bath at the Roman level, or jump in. The Pump Room's columned front,
      with "water is best" in Greek along its frieze, faces the Abbey.
    - **Great Pulteney Street**: Baldwin's long Georgian street from Laura Place, both sides one
      design: rusticated ground floors with fanlit doors and railings, tall windows under a
      modillion cornice, pavilions with fluted Corinthian pilasters and a pediment, and slate
      mansards with dormers and chimneys, block by block between the side streets.
    - **The Holburne Museum** at its far end, looking straight down it: the three-arched loggia
      you can walk up into, its Corinthian portico, urns along the parapet, and the glass and
      ceramic extension in Sydney Gardens behind.
    - **Argyle Street**, on from the bridge to Laura Place: a shopfront to every house, with the
      names of the shops there now over them.
    - **Grand Parade**, along the river from the bridge: Davis's colonnade and river wall below
      it, and a level pavement to the balustrade along the top, with its urns and cast-iron lamps
      that light up after dark.
    - **The Empire Hotel** on the corner of Orange Grove: its octagonal tower under a lead dome,
      oriels running up the front, and the roofline facing the river: a castle, a house with two
      gables and a cottage.
    - **Sham Castle**, up on Bathampton Down looking over the city: Ralph Allen's battlemented
      folly, all front, with its turrets either side of an arch you can walk through.
    - **Prior Park**, above Combe Down: Ralph Allen's Palladian mansion by John Wood, fifteen bays
      on a rusticated basement under a balustrade and a hipped slate roof, the giant portico of
      six Corinthian columns looking down the valley to Bath, and the broad flight of steps you
      can climb from the lawn onto it. The drive side has its own pedimented centre and door. Its
      wings step down the hill either side, joined to it by low arcaded links.
    - **Kingswood School**, up on Lansdown: Wilson's long Tudor Gothic range of 1852 with its
      battlements and the tall tower over the entrance, pinnacled at its corners, looking east over
      the valley, and the chapel below it with its pointed windows, buttresses and gabled ends.

Full screen is in the pause menu (Esc, or II on a touch screen). In Chrome and Edge, Esc then only
pauses, and you hold Esc down to leave full screen; it stays full screen down the mines and in the
deli too. Safari and Firefox still leave full screen when you press Esc.

Settings, on the main menu and in the pause menu, has everything you can change in one place, and
it's all remembered on that computer for next time. Picture: draw distance (how far you can see,
from 600 m to 3 km), resolution (fewer lines if it runs slowly on a big or Retina screen, from
Lowest, about 270, through Normal, about 540, to Full), field of view (60 to 100 degrees), shadows,
the retro look, and the debug panel. Controls: mouse sensitivity. Play: passive mode (also a tickbox on the main menu), where nothing
comes for anyone: cats, naughty boys, the rugby and cricket teams, the Flag Lads, Bendy and seagull
muck all leave you alone, even if you shoot at them (other players can still shoot you, and the
traffic and trains can still run you over). It's for the whole game, and the host decides: whoever
starts a game brings their setting, everyone who joins plays by it, and if the host changes theirs
mid-game it changes for everyone. Sound: music, sound effects and voice chat volumes. Reset to
defaults puts them all back.

It's a big map, so the pause menu has Go to a pub: a map of the whole
force field with a pint on every pub in it, about a hundred across Bath. Scroll or pinch to zoom
and drag to look about; point at a pub for its name, how far it is and its food hygiene rating from
the Food Standards Agency where there is one, and click it to be on the pavement outside, facing
the door (click where the pubs crowd together, in the middle of town, and the map zooms in). You
can't go while you're dead or within five seconds of being hurt, so it's no way out of a fight.
Once you've been to a pub, that's where you come back to life when you're killed.

Every game day a fresh set of jobs goes up round Bath, on noticeboards at real places: a few near
the village and the rest out across town, each a badge on the minimap and the maps. Walk up to one
to read it and press E (Use on a touch screen) to take it on; everyone in the game is on the same
job at once, with a card at the top left saying how it's going, and the reward drops where it
finishes. Everyone gets the same noticeboards in the same places that day, and a job you've done
stays ticked off till the next. Call off a job from the pause menu.

Today's jobs: when you join, a panel shows three little missions for the day (shoot some flags
down, win a pub back from the Flag Lads, ride a couple of kilometres, land a combo, get clocked by a
speed trap, have a pint...) and how the Flag Lads are doing. It tucks away after a few seconds: hold
Tab to see it again over the league table, or pause on a touch screen. They're the same three for
everyone that day, a new three each midnight, and your progress is kept on your device. Each one
ticks off with a cheer as you finish it.

The Flag Lads are out to cable-tie an England flag to every lamp post in Bath, whether Bath likes it
or not. They're made up, and the joke's on them: on the flag-planting, the "taking our streets
back" (from Swindon, mostly) and the "I'm not being funny, but..." that's never far behind, not on
the flag itself. How they're doing is in today's jobs (below): how much of Bath they've flagged,
who the Gaffer is, and any pub they've taken. While you're in a square with flags up, while there's a takeover on, or for a
minute after you shoot a flag down, a meter comes up under the minimap ("Flags up 27%") with how many you've had down,
the takeover's next step, and the square you're in ("This square: 10/22 flags down"). The full map
washes their squares red. A square flies about 30 flags at most; shoot every one and it's
cleared, and it stays clear unless the squares next door fill up. Finding the last few is up to
you, but you can hear the nearest one flapping once you're close.
Where they're in charge the street lamps fly the flag, with red and white bunting strung between
them once a square's mostly taken. The Flag Lads themselves (England shirts, some with the flag
worn as a cape, waving little flags) don't hang about the streets: you only meet them at the pubs
they've taken over. Shoot a flag and it
tears in two and flutters down, and the square drops a little; ladder lads in hi-vis come to put
them back up and run from you rather than fight, and catching one stops the ladders round there for
a while. Squares nobody's in creep up, and a square that's mostly taken spreads next door. Every few
minutes the phone goes: the Flag Lads have taken over a pub. A purple marker goes up; get there
before the clock runs out and clear them out (the meter counts down the lads left, and says when
it's the Gaffer's turn), with bonus prizes if you do it inside 90 seconds. The crew and the Gaffer
grow with the number of you in the room, and with five or more they take two pubs at once (the
second run by the Gaffer's cousin) so you have to split up. There's one Gaffer at a time, with a
silly name and a trait or two: a pop-up gazebo that soaks up shots while he stands under it, a
megaphone that brings more lads, a right-hand man who has to go down first, or the whole WhatsApp
group (a bigger crew). The Gaffer remembers: win a pub off you and he comes back tougher with a new
trait and a taunt about it; beat him and his successor swears revenge. A pub won back flies an ALL
WELCOME banner, turns green on the map, leaves a ride outside, puts a free round out every couple of
minutes while you're near, and keeps the Flag Lads out of that square for the rest of the day.
Leave a takeover and the lads dig in, and that square and the ones round it jump up. Get the meter
to 0% and Bath is flag-free till tomorrow: every day the Flag Lads start again from
a few new squares, picking up wherever the clock says they should have got to.

Some of the jobs are Wanted posters: a ringleader with a silly made-up title ("the Duke of
Disorder") hiding out with his crew (naughty boys, a rugby team or cricket club gone bad, or a
breakaway lot of Flag Lads). The poster says the one loud thing he wears (a red rosette,
a hi-vis tabard or a big purple hat, and nobody else round there has one), his routine (a pub, then
a park or square, then a bus stop, round and round, so you can lie in wait at his next stop) and two
quirks: slippery (shoot at him from afar and he bolts, so corner him first; everyone gets a marker on him
as he runs, waiting at the stop he runs to), lookouts (if they see
you first, someone phones ahead and he moves) or mob-handed (never out without a gang, and more
turn up once it kicks off). There's a hidden third, which the first clue gives away: hard as nails,
stubborn (he won't come quietly) or a bus pass (he gets to his next stop before you). A search
circle 300 m across goes on the map with him somewhere inside, and so do clues: a witness to ask or
a leaflet he's dropped (E). Each one shrinks the circle and closes it in on him, for everyone, and
knocking out a lookout counts too. Get a look at him up close, or through binoculars or a scope from
further off (further still from a roof or a hill), and everyone gets a marker on him and his name on
the boss bar, and the spotter gets a prize. Put him down for the prizes and a ride, or take him in
for more: wear him down with someone close by and he gives up with his hands in the air. Cuff him
(E) and he follows you to the police at the nearest bus stop or police box while his mates try to
spring him; get too far ahead and he slips off to be cuffed again. Each bounty is six to eight
minutes. Run out of time and he slips the net, but only once: the circle opens up again, wider,
round his next stop, with a couple more minutes on the clock.

Treasure trails are a chain of four to six riddles, each about a real pub, church, park, garden,
square or street in Bath ("Where the Hadley Arms crowd drinks", "Bells ring for Saint Mary"), with a
biscuit tin on a post somewhere near it that you can get to: open it (E) for the next riddle. The
riddles get harder with the stars: the name plainly, then with letters missing, then jumbled or as a
cryptic hint. Now and then the riddle is a picture instead, a little sepia sketch of the view from
near the tin, drawn once you're close enough for that part of Bath to be loaded. Each leg comes with
its own ride (a BMX, rollerblades, a pogo stick...), handed to you as the riddle opens, so a trail
is a tour of 10 to 15 minutes too. Within 400 m a search circle goes on the map, and it shrinks in
steps as you close in, with a chime (and a colder one if you wander off). Stuck for two minutes, E
gives you a hint that shrinks the circle, but it costs score and the gold. The last tin is the Bath
Bun chest, buried in a mound: dig it up and coins burst out, and you get a gold, silver or bronze
rosette, pinned on your jacket for everyone to see (your best three are kept). Some trails you do
together, where anyone's find moves everyone on, and with two or more of you some legs have twin
tins at two places that must both be opened within 30 seconds, so you have to split up. Some are
races, where everyone has their own copy and the first to the chest wins. Each has a par time, for
gold, silver or bronze, and one trail a day is the trail of the day: the same for everyone in every
room, with your best time kept, so you can compare.

Races go from the noticeboard to a real place 1 to 3 km away, on a ride that suits the lie of the
land along the way: a BMX (or a racing bike, if it's long) for a steep climb, the skateboard or a
shopping trolley for a run that only goes down, rollerblades or the scooter on the flat, and a pogo
dash for a short flat hop. Taking one puts everyone on the start line with the ride, held still by a
countdown that's the same for everyone, then it's any way you like: there's just a start and a
finish, with the marker and an arrow over the crosshair pointing at the finish, so finding the
shortcuts is the skill. Splits at three points on the way flash green or red against your best,
there's gold, silver and bronze (and a hidden author's time to beat), and you race see-through
riders of your best run and the fastest runs the others in the room have sent round, alongside the
real riders. Backspace (Again on a touch screen) puts just you back on the line for a quick 3-2-1
of your own, as often as you like and for two minutes after a race is over, while the room's result
stands. Each day there's a daily race round the village too, the same for everyone, with a board of
the times in the room and the local legend: whoever's had the most goes at it.

Seagull Attack: tourists sat on benches with their sandwiches, and the gulls of Bath want them.
Take the job and a ring of benches is set out near the noticeboard, three tourists with a sandwich
each (one more tourist for each extra player), then the gulls come in off the roofs in five waves,
each bigger and more mixed than the last. Herring gulls circle and swoop (watch for their shadow
growing on the ground): shoot them on the way in. Hoverers hang over a bench, then drop straight
down: keep your crosshair on one for a second and a half to stare it out. A gang of five
black-headed gulls is too many to shoot, so shout at them (E) and everything near you scatters.
Walkers land out of sight and stroll up behind the benches, and poopers fly over dropping on
people: muck on you splatters your screen and slows you down. A gull that grabs a bite flies off
to the roofs with it, and shooting it before it gets there saves the bite. The last wave brings
Big Lad, a seagull the size of a bus: he sits on the highest roof, flies over dropping poo bombs
(a white ring shows where each will land), dives for a whole sandwich unless he's shot enough to
pull out, and calls in a gang when he's half beaten; his beak's the weak spot. Between waves, pick
one of three crates by the benches: a bucket of chips to throw down as a decoy, a plastic owl, a
brolly, a megaphone or second helpings. Stars for how much sandwich is left, a score to beat (your
best at each spot is kept), a twist each day (a bank holiday, Chip Tuesday, a hen do, bin day),
and Backspace for another go on your own.

The wardrobe: daft things to wear, earned round Bath, and seen by everyone you play with. A
seagull sitting on your head for three stars on a Seagull Attack, a splattered flat cap for being
pooed on five times, shades and a sunburnt nose for winning one, a plastic owl or a splattered
brolly for beating Big Lad cleanly, a traffic cone for being first over the line in a race, a
centurion's helmet for gold on a treasure trail, a deerstalker for bringing in a Wanted ringleader,
joke glasses and a moustache for your first job, a chef's hat for three stars at the Darling Deli
and a baguette in a rucksack for ten shifts there, a cat on your shoulder for living through a cat
swarm, and a baby's bonnet for ten minutes of tummy time. One thing on your head, one on your face
and one on your back: pick them in the Wardrobe in the pause menu, which shows you turning round in
them. What you've earned is kept in this browser.

Free play on the rides: speed traps (yellow boxes on poles, marked on the big map as yellow speed
cameras, and on the minimap when you're within a walk) stand at the foot of the steepest streets in Bath and clock you as you ride through, and a manual down
Milsom Street is timed. Both keep your best.

Rides turn up round the streets, in pink on the minimap: a racing bike, a BMX, rollerblades, a skateboard, a
scooter, a pogo stick, and shopping trolleys outside the shops. Walk over one to have it and press
X (Ride on a touch screen) to get on or off; you keep it till you pick up another, and the ride you've
got shows above your rounds, bottom right. On a bike, a BMX or a scooter you see its handlebars in front of
you, turning as you steer. Each goes its own
way: A and D steer and W and S speed up and slow down, faster on the pavement and the road than on
grass. Bath's hills are the whole game: downhill you pick up speed till the wind roars and the view
widens, and uphill it bleeds away however hard you push. Each ride has one thing it does on Space
(Jump on a touch screen): the racing bike tucks down for the hills, the BMX bunny hops (hold to
crouch, let go to hop), the skateboard ollies, the rollerblades jump, the scooter kicks for a burst,
the pogo stick does a big bounce (press it just as you land for a perfect one, higher still), and
you hop on the back of a trolley and hold on, which goes like a rocket downhill. Space again in the
air does the ride's trick (a kickflip, a tailwhip). Come down near a kerb, a low wall, a railing or
a parked car running your way and you snap on and grind along it; Shift (Trick) grinds one you're
alongside, or else holds a manual on the flat, kept up with W and S. Airs, spins, tricks, grinds
and manuals chain into a combo, its score in a small counter mid-screen; roll along on the ground
for a moment and it's banked, with your bests on that ride (longest grind, biggest air, longest
manual, best combo) kept in your browser. Landings straighten themselves if you're near enough lined
up (on a touch screen, always), with a bonus for a clean one after a spin or a trick. Come down
sideways, mid-trick or off the end of a manual and you bail: a quick tumble, the combo's gone, and
you're back on in under two seconds. Only a really hard crash into a wall hurts, and not much.
Someone pushing a trolley can give a mate a lift: they walk up to the basket and press E to climb
in, and can look about and shoot from it.

Ladders turn up round the streets too (white on the minimap), and a ladder lad always leaves his
behind when you put him down. Walk over one to carry it, over your shoulder where everyone can see.
Face the wall of a building and press E (Use on a touch screen) and it runs up to the edge of the
roof, up to 15 metres. Then anyone can climb it: walk into its foot and W takes you up, and at the
top you step off onto the roof. To come down, walk off the roof's edge over the top of it (facing
away from the wall, W goes down; facing it, S does), and Space lets go. E at the foot of a ladder
takes it down to carry on, whoever's up top. One left with nobody about goes after a few minutes,
and if you go down carrying one, it's left lying where you fell.

Day turns to night and back again every 24 minutes: the sun comes up in the east and goes down
in the west with a warm glow round it, then the stars and the moon come out, the street lamps
light the pavements round them and windows light up across Bath. The traffic, the buses and
anything someone's driving put their headlights on, throwing a beam down the road ahead (so you
see a car coming round a corner by the light on the walls), with their tail lights glowing red;
they come on in tunnels too. On a computer you can see about 900 metres by day (from Alexandra
Park, out over the city) and the lamps' glow from right across town at night; phones and iPads
see a little over half as far, to keep them smooth. Everyone sees the same time of day, since it
comes from the clock rather than the host. Cats keep to the residential streets, among the houses
rather than in town by the shops and pubs, big and small, the odd kitten or fat one among them, and
long-haired ones of most breeds. Shoot one and the few nearest come for you, with a
handful more trickling in. They're faster than you, but they won't follow you far: get 80 m from
where it started (the screen counts down the metres) and they give up and wander off. Once that's
over you get 30 seconds' grace before another lot will. After dark the cats get braver: more of
them are out, the calm ones come and sit round your feet, and shoot one and half as many again
come for you. To see a time of day, add ?time=21:30 to the address (the clock stops there), or
?daylen=4 for a four-minute day; with the debug panel open, T (or its "+3 hours" button) moves the clock on three hours for everyone in your game. L (or
the draw distance button) steps how far you can see through 600, 900, 1200, 1500, 2000 and 3000 metres, to
find what your machine can keep smooth, and is remembered like the one in Settings; ?view=1200 in the
address tries a distance for one visit.

F3 (or Debug info in the pause menu, on a phone or iPad) opens a debug panel: where you are, in the
game's coordinates and as a real place (latitude and longitude and an OS grid reference), which way
you're facing and which square of Bath you're in; the frame rate and draw calls; and how your link
to each player is doing (direct or through the relay, round trip, lost voice packets, who's
talking). Shift+F3 or its Copy button copies your position. Add ?debug=1 to the address to open it
from the start.

The roads, paths, canal and railways cross each other on proper bridges: every bridge has a deck
with Bath-stone parapets, abutments where it leaves the ground, piers along a long span and
arches where it stands high enough, with the river, the railway or the road it crosses running
on underneath (Pulteney Bridge is Robert Adam's own, and the Dundas Aqueduct carries the canal
over the Avon and the railway). Roads rise and fall like roads rather than following every bump
of the survey, sit in cuttings and on embankments with sloping sides, and the river banks slope
down to the water instead of dropping off a cliff. Garden and field walls the Environment Agency
lidar sees stand at their measured height, carried on where they pass under a tree, and streets
are as wide as OpenStreetMap and the walls either side of them allow, with a pavement only where
there is one (or where a footpath runs alongside, the footpath). Every garden has its own front
wall, with its gate set in it and a path up to the door, and one straight fence or hedge between it
and each neighbour, joined up at the corners and kept off the footpaths; a real wall takes the place of a guessed one, and walls are opened only where a path
or a street crosses them. A wall runs on as one piece and stops where it meets another, nothing grows in
a gateway or on a garden path, and footpaths lie on the ground. The pavements stand a kerb high.
Jump at a wall up to 2.6 m high and you pull yourself up onto it.

A second map, Royal Victoria Park, is set up in `tools/area.py` but left out of the game for now:
build it as below and add it back to `web/src/maps.ts` to try it. A test map outside Bath, Hackney
(a kilometre round the Pembury Tavern in east London), has its own page, `pembury.html`, and is
left out of the main menu: London stock-brick terraces and council estates, built the same way from
Overture and the Environment Agency lidar.

Naughty boys hang about in gangs in alleys and at bus stops, and now and then on a street corner, all over Bath, Fox Hill most of all. Get spotted and they chase you: they're faster than a walk
but slower than a sprint, and they give up if you get far enough away or out of sight. If they
catch you they hit you. Shooting anyone, player or naughty boy, splatters them in your paint: it splashes
on the ground round them and they leave painted footprints for a few seconds.

Up at the top of town, within 100 m of 1 Fonthill Road, lives Bendy: four metres of black, rubbery
limbs with a pale grinning face and a lamp in his forehead. He only comes out at night: he rises
once it's dark, and at first light he sinks into the ground, even mid-chase, till dusk. He walks his patch slowly, sweeping the
land with his lamp's beam like a lighthouse. Get caught in the beam and he screeches, the beam goes
red and locks on you, and then he comes: he sinks into the ground and pops up right beside you,
lashes out with his long arms, and does it again and again, so running doesn't help. Shoot him and
he comes for you. He takes over a hundred rifle rounds to put down (under fifty in the head), so bring
friends; a roof is out of his reach, and get far enough from his patch and he gives up and goes
home. When he goes down he melts into a puddle of ink and leaves three of the best prizes behind,
and a few minutes later he's back. While he's near, his health shows across the top of the screen,
and he's a purple dot on the map.

You start with a rifle and 120 spare rounds. Keys 1, 2 and 3 pick your fists, the rifle and the
sniper rifle, and right click toggles aiming. Aiming the paintball marker brings its red dot sight
up to your eye, and the paint goes where the dot is. The sniper rifle has 10-round magazines, a bolt to
work between shots and a scope that sways. The middle of Firs Field is the special spot: each time
it fills it's either a sniper rifle or a bottle of Co-op Bucks Fizz, which fills you right up. The
Hadley Arms and the King William always have a pint outside (40 health). Three more sniper rifles,
tins of rifle rounds, boxes of sniper rounds and the odd pint turn up outside shops and pubs and on
street corners near you, wherever you are in Bath, and naughty boys sometimes drop a pint, a bottle or some rounds. Picking up a second sniper
rifle, a tin or a box adds a spare magazine. Run dry and it's fists.

It's paintball by default. The rifle and the sniper rifle are paintball markers, with a hopper of
paintballs on top and an air tank for a stock, and they fire paintballs you can watch fly. The
balls in your hopper are loose, one for each shot left: they roll about as you tilt the gun, slosh
when you run and jump, rattle up every time you fire, go down as you shoot and tumble back in when
you reload. Each
player's paint is their own colour: it bursts where it lands and leaves splats on walls and the
ground, and goes round the edge of your screen when you're hit. There's no blood. For the real
guns and blood (sprays off every hit, blood up the wall behind whoever you shoot, a red mist off
headshots and sniper rounds, and big pools), add `?gore=1` to the address (`?gore=0` to go back), or type `claret` while you're
playing (again to go back). The browser remembers it, so it carries over into rooms you join and
the mines, and it's only what you see: everyone else keeps their own setting.

Pitches are marked out for the sport they're mapped for: football pitches with goals and nets,
rugby pitches with H posts, and cricket fields with a wicket and a boundary rope. Now and then, on
any of these anywhere in Bath (Twerton Park's included), a ball turns up for you (a white dot on the map). On a football pitch
it's on the penalty spot, or out on the edge of the box for a harder one: put it in the net, under
the bar. On a rugby pitch it's on a kicking tee in front of the posts, sometimes an easy one and
sometimes a long one from out wide; on a cricket field it's at the bowler's end of the wicket, to bowl at the
stumps at the other end (pitch it short and it bounces over them, too full and it's over the bails,
and one that bounces more than once doesn't count). Walk up to it to line up: A and D aim, W and S set the power, Space or a click kicks or
bowls it and E walks off (on a touch screen: the stick, Fire and Jump). Dots trace the start of the ball's
flight as it would go on a still day, but each ball has its own wind (shown under the power bar) and
you have to aim off to allow for it. Score a goal, kick it over the bar between the uprights, or
knock the stumps over, and a prize lands in the goalmouth, under the posts or behind the stumps:
Bucks Fizz, a sniper rifle, rounds, or a bottle of Lucozade, which doubles your damage for 30
seconds. A long kick from out wide, or a goal from the edge of the box, gets one of the better ones. Once the ball's gone, the telly takes over: a
chase camera and a shot from behind the posts for a kick, the long lens from behind the bowler's arm
and a stump-level shot for a ball, in letterbox and slow motion at the moment that matters. Where
there's a terrace or a wall right behind the goal, as at Twerton Park, the camera behind the posts
goes up above it, in behind the net or out beside the goal instead. Any key
or a click goes back to your own eyes.

The cars parked along The Firs (by Firs Field) and Fox Hill have their keys in: Ford Escort XR3is,
Reliant Robins, Vauxhall Novas, Austin Metros, Ford Sierras, a Police Sierra (with a siren and
flashing lights), a Transit van, a Mr Whippy van (playing Greensleeves), a milk float and a Sinclair
C5. Badgerline double-deckers and minibuses wait at bus stops, and every so often a bus nobody's
using turns up at another stop. Or call one: stand at any stop in Bath, press E, then 1 for a minibus or 2
for a double-decker, and a Badgerline driver brings one down the street (a green dot on the map)
and gets off at the kerb for you; each cricket club has its heavy roller out. Walk up to one and press
E (or Get in on a touch screen): you take the wheel if it's free, otherwise a passenger seat, where
you can look about and shoot (the bus's top deck is the place to be). Drivers get a chase camera: W
and S drive and brake, A and D steer, Space is the handbrake, H or a click sounds the horn (or
switches the siren or the chimes on and off), and E gets you out. The front wheels turn with the
steering and the car turns about its back axle, so it sweeps round a corner on its turning circle
(the traffic drives the same way) rather than pivoting on the spot. Pull the handbrake with some lock on
and the back end comes round for a donut; the back wheels leave black rubber on the road and send up
clouds of tyre smoke whenever they lock or slide, and the marks fade after a minute or so. Vehicles block like walls and
rounds go through their windows, so you can shoot the driver. Anyone you hit at speed goes under,
the heavier the vehicle the worse, and the roller flattens people at a crawl. Take a Robin round a
corner too fast and it goes over; drive the milk float into something and the bottles go. The
traffic gives way when you drive into it, and so do the cars parked along every street in Bath and
any vehicle nobody's in: you lose a bit of speed and plough on, and the car you hit is knocked
spinning off its line or out of its parking space (a bus barely notices, a C5 bounces off). T-bone one hard and it
trips over its wheels and rolls; if it comes to rest on its roof it catches fire and goes up a
moment later. The fireball hurts any naughty boys, rugby lads, cricketers or Bendy near it (never players,
and never the cats), counts as your kill, and throws the rest of the traffic about, which can set
off another. Some gangs of naughty boys arrive in a Nova and pile out. The host says who sits where, and everyone sees the
vehicles where their drivers left them (`web/src/vehicles.ts`).

On Bathwick Street, outside No. 23 Chinese Kitchen, a BMW X5 has broken down at the kerb: bonnet
up, hazards flashing, steam coming off the engine, and a yellow Dick Lovett Recovery truck pulled in ahead of it.
Neither of them is going anywhere. Its owner paces the pavement in his gilet, on the phone, head in
his hands, telling anyone who comes near all about it (seventy grand, and it won't start). He can't
be hurt: shoot him and he just complains about that as well (`web/src/breakdown.ts`).

The Kennet and Avon Canal runs round the east of the map, from the River Avon at Widcombe up the
six locks of the Widcombe flight (Bath Deep Lock is nearly six metres), through the two short
tunnels in Sydney Gardens, out past Bathampton and Claverton and over the Dundas Aqueduct, with the
Somerset Coal Canal's arm off to Brassknocker Basin. Its water sits at the right level all the way,
and it's lined with moored narrowboats. The locks work: walk up to one and press E (Use on a touch
screen) to lean on a balance beam, and the gates swing shut, the water roars through the paddles
and the chamber fills or empties, then the gates at the other end open. Narrowboats chug along,
keeping right of each other, and wait at each lock while their crew works it. Hop aboard and you
ride along on the roof; walk to the back and press E to take the tiller: W and S work the throttle
(it stays where you leave it), A and D put the tiller over, and E lets go. The rudder's at the
stern, so the boat only answers while it's moving, and slowly: it swings its bow round and keeps
turning until you bring the tiller back, and it sets you over towards the bank until you
straighten up, so steer early and straighten early. A tiller held over at a junction picks that
branch.
Take a boat up the flight and you work the locks yourself. The host runs the locks and boats
(`web/src/canal.ts`, laid out by `tools/canal.py`).

The railways run too: InterCity 125s on Brunel's Great Western main line, along the viaduct through
Bath Spa and on through Oldfield Park and the Twerton tunnels, and Sprinters down the Limpley Stoke
valley line that joins it at Bathampton, stopping at the stations. Trains keep to the clock, so
everyone sees the same ones; they sound their horns at anyone on the line ahead and run down anyone
who stays there. Get up on the roof of one and it takes you with it (`web/src/railway.ts`, laid out
by `tools/railway.py`). The lines are on the minimap and the big map, dashed like a railway on an
Ordnance Survey map.

The cable car Curo proposed in 2017, and dropped after a public consultation, runs from Mulberry
Park down to the river at Bath Spa. Two big cabins shuttle on their own ropes, one going down as the
other comes up, out over Lyncombe Vale to a tower on Beechen Cliff and down over Holloway. Walk into
a cabin while its doors are open at either station and it takes you across in a couple of minutes,
or stand on its roof for the view. The cabins keep to the clock like the trains, so everyone sees
the same ones (`web/src/cableway.ts`, laid out by `tools/cableway.py`).

**COMBE DOWN WITH ME** is a co-op cooking game in the kitchen of the Darling Deli, for one to four
cooks (`web/deli.html`, `web/src/deli/`). Walk up to the deli's front door, under the orange neon
sign, and press E (Go in on a touch screen), or pick it from the front page. You all work one
three-minute shift: tickets come in along the top, and you fetch food from the crates, chop loaves,
cheddar and tomatoes on the boards, fry bacon and eggs on the hobs, bake Bath bun dough in the oven,
put it together on a plate and hand it through the pass. The menu is a Bath Bun, a Bacon Butty, a
Ploughman's, a Cheese & Tomato Sarnie and The Full Combe Down. Anything left on the heat burns (bin
it), quick service earns a bigger tip, and customers who wait too long walk out. Takings earn up to
three stars. WASD or the arrows walk, Space or E picks up and puts down, and holding F or Shift
chops; on a touch screen you walk with your thumb anywhere on the left and use the Grab and Chop
buttons. On a phone held upright the kitchen turns to fit. The party name carries over from the
street, and leaving puts you back outside the deli. Cooks can talk to each other: your mic is open
in the kitchen and everyone hears everyone, with a switch for it on the menu.

Other players wear a jacket in their own colour, with their own face, hair or beanie, trousers and
shoes. Their head turns and tilts to look wherever they're looking, they hold their gun up along
the same line (it kicks when they fire), their legs walk, run, crouch and tuck up in a jump, they
blink, and their mouth moves while they talk.

Press Z (or tap Crouch twice on a touch screen) to lie on your front. You crawl along slowly with
your eyes just off the ground and you're much harder to hit, but to everyone else you're a baby
doing tummy time: flat on a patchwork play mat, propped up on your forearms with a big wobbly
head, a dummy in and a rattle in your hand, legs kicking away. Press Z again, or jump, to get up.

Press F (or the Torch button on touch screens) to switch your torch on or off. It lights up
wherever you look, and everyone else can see your beam too, swinging round as you turn.

Online games have voice chat. Your mic is open to players nearby: they hear you in 3D from where
you stand, fading out by 60 m. Hold V (or the Radio button on touch screens) to talk to everyone
in the room on the radio, through a crackly walkie-talkie filter with a squelch and an "over" beep.
The browser asks for the microphone when you press Play, before the game takes the mouse, and you can mute it from the pause menu (switching it back on there asks again if it was refused). If someone can't hear you, the game puts your voice back on their link within a few seconds.
If someone in the room is on a different version of the game (voice chat needs everyone on the
same one), a small note at the top of the screen says who should refresh; tap it to refresh or hide it.
If your connection to another player drops, the two of you look for each other again straight away
and are back within a few seconds.

Joining a game that's already running, you wait on the menu ("Connecting to 2 of 3 players…")
until you've linked up with everyone playing, so you don't arrive to find nobody there. If someone
can't be reached after 15 seconds, you go in with whoever you have.

The sound effects and the soundtrack (procedural 90s-shooter metal, `web/src/music.ts`) are
synthesised in the browser, so the game ships no audio files.

The look is a modern take on 90s Build-engine shooters: chunky pixel-art textures drawn in the
browser (`web/src/textures.ts`), leafy swaying trees and shrubs (`web/src/trees.ts`), tufts of grass
with buttercups and daisies around you (`web/src/grass.ts`), and a punchy colour grade with a fine
palette dither over the frame, drawn at about 540 lines (or what Resolution in Settings
sets) and scaled up in crisp square pixels (`web/src/post.ts`). Retro look in Settings turns the grade off; for one visit, add `?fx=0` to the URL to turn it off, or `?res=full` for full resolution.

Multiplayer is peer to peer (WebRTC via [Trystero](https://github.com/dmotz/trystero)): you type a
game name (like `HADLEY`) and press Play. If that game is running you join it, on its map; if not,
you start it on the map you picked. If everyone in that game has gone down the mines, it says so
and offers to take you down after them. Tell your friends the name, or share the link, and browsers
connect directly. There is no game server.
The big map (M, or tap the minimap) zooms in with the mouse wheel, + and −, or a pinch, and drags
about when zoomed (with the mouse captured, push the crosshair against its edge). Each job's badge
has its name under it there. In its top left corner is a key saying what everything on the maps is;
click a row (or put the crosshair on it and click) to take that kind of thing off the minimap, the
big map and the pub map, and again to bring it back. The Key button folds it away. Both are kept in
the browser for next time.
When someone joins or leaves, a notice pops up in the middle of the screen above the street sign.
The league table (hold Tab) also shows the game's name, map and how many are playing.
Everyone else in the game shows on the minimap and the big map as an arrow
in their colour, pointing the way they face, with their name on the big map and a red ring round
them while they're shooting. On the minimap, anyone out of range sits on the rim in their direction.
The ways into the minigames, the mine tours and COMBE DOWN WITH ME, are marked as points of
interest: a white star in a cyan ring on the minimap, the big map and the pub map (within 800 m the
minimap keeps it on its rim, pointing the way), and in the street the same marker over the door and
a ring of light on the step.
The cable car is on the minimap, the big map and the pub map too: a red and white line between its
two stations, with a cable car badge at each end.

To show everyone where to go, put a marker down: click or tap anywhere on the big map (with the
mouse captured, move the crosshair and click), or press G while looking at a spot. Each player has one marker, in their player colour, the same
for everyone, so "the blue marker" means the same one to all; putting it somewhere else moves it, and tapping it again on the big map (or pressing G
while looking at it) takes it down. Everyone in the game sees it as a pin on the minimap and the
big map, a ring of light on the ground at the spot in the street, and a pin on the compass
strip across the top of the screen with how far away it is. A marker behind you sits at the end of
the strip with an arrow saying which way to turn. It goes when its owner leaves.

## Rebuild a map

Combe Down and the rest of Bath are one map, built in full-detail squares about a kilometre
across (`tools/prep_tiles.py`, see docs/new-area.md). The village is the middle 3 x 3 of them,
built in the same run as the rest and then stitched into one file the game loads first
(`web/public/maps/combe-down.json`); the other squares are streamed in round the player. A lighter
whole-city file goes underneath, for the minimap and for anywhere a square isn't loaded:

Everything the map is built from is kept in the repository, in `data/combe-down/`
(`tools/mapdata.py`), so it rebuilds from a fresh clone with nothing to download, and comes out
byte for byte the map everyone else gets: the Overture, OpenStreetMap and food hygiene data, the
energy certificates (without addresses), the terrain tiles, and what the build measures from the
Environment Agency lidar and the tree canopy map (building heights, roofs and the canopy, square by
square), which are gigabytes themselves. It needs Python 3 with numpy, shapely and Pillow:

```sh
pip3 install numpy shapely pillow
MAP=combe-down
python3 -I tools/mapdata.py unpack $MAP        # the repository's copy -> data/raw/$MAP/wide
# the canal and the railways first: the squares cut the ground under the track and the canal to
# the levels these work out
python3 -I tools/canal.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-canal.json
python3 -I tools/railway.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-rail.json
python3 -I tools/prep_tiles.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP --force
python3 -I tools/prep_wide.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-wide.json
python3 -I tools/pubs.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-pubs.json
```

That takes about 5 minutes on four cores, and a second run only rebuilds the squares whose data or tools have changed. On GitHub, the "Rebuild the map" workflow (Actions tab, Run workflow) does the same across ten machines at once, commits the map files and redeploys the game. A change to the build tools is rebuilt the same way; a
change to what is measured from the lidar or the canopy (`tools/lidar.py`, `tools/canopy.py`,
`buildings.roof_parts`) needs the raw data, measured afresh. To fetch it all from the public
sources again (about 2.5 GB, plus the energy certificates by hand), measure it and pack it back
into `data/combe-down/`:

```sh
pip3 install overturemaps rasterio
python3 -I tools/fetch.py $MAP                  # -> data/raw/$MAP/wide (see the top of fetch.py)
python3 -I tools/canal.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-canal.json
python3 -I tools/railway.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-rail.json
python3 -I tools/prep_tiles.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP --force --measure
python3 -I tools/prep_wide.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-wide.json --measure
python3 -I tools/pubs.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide web/public/maps/$MAP-pubs.json
python3 -I tools/mapdata.py pack $MAP data/raw/$MAP/wide
```

With only the lidar and the canopy tile fetched (`tools/fetch.py $MAP --only lidar,canopy`, after
`mapdata.py unpack`), the same `--measure` builds measure afresh from the data already in the
repository. `data/combe-down/README.md` says where each part comes from and its licence.

A map with no wide area (Royal Victoria Park) is one file, built straight from its own downloads
with `python3 -I tools/prep_map.py $MAP data/raw/$MAP web/public/maps/$MAP.json`.

Each map's centre, size and local character areas are in `tools/area.py`. To make a map of a new
area, follow [docs/new-area.md](docs/new-area.md): every download, where it goes, the build, and
landmark packs.

### Building heights and roofs from lidar (optional)

With Environment Agency lidar, each building gets its real eaves height, ridge height and roof
shape (gabled, hipped or flat, and which way the ridge runs) instead of a guess from its area
(`tools/lidar.py`). Download the 1 m **LIDAR Composite DTM**, **LIDAR Composite First Return
DSM** and **LIDAR Composite Last Return DSM** from https://environment.data.gov.uk/survey for the
OS squares the map covers (Combe Down: ST76SE and ST76SW) and unzip the GeoTIFFs into the map's
raw folder:

```sh
data/raw/combe-down/lidar/dtm/*.tif
data/raw/combe-down/lidar/dsm/*.tif
data/raw/combe-down/lidar/last/*.tif
pip3 install rasterio
```

Buildings the survey doesn't show (newer than the flight) keep the rule-based guess, and so do
roofs mostly under trees: where the first and last returns differ, the laser went through leaves.
Without the last returns, the canopy map says where the trees are instead.

### Tree canopy (optional)

Trees are placed from Meta / WRI's global canopy height map when a tile is present in
`data/raw/<map>/canopy/`. For this map that is the zoom-9 quadkey tile `031313120`:

```sh
mkdir -p data/raw/combe-down/canopy
curl -o data/raw/combe-down/canopy/031313120.tif \
  https://dataforgood-fb-data.s3.amazonaws.com/forests/v1/alsgedi_global_v6_float/chm/031313120.tif
pip3 install rasterio
```
