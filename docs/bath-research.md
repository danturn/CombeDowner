## 1. City-wide building conventions

### Stone
- **Bath stone** is an oolitic limestone (Great Oolite), honey to cream in colour, and it is the default walling material across the whole city. Bath Preservation Trust (BPT) describes Bath stone ashlar plus natural slate as the material that gives the World Heritage Site its "visual homogeneity".
- **Ashlar and rubble:** front elevations are smooth ashlar. Rear walls and side returns are often coursed rubble. Examples:
  - c1815 London Road terrace: "ashlar facade, rubblestone rear; slate to the front, concrete tile to the rear".
  - c1760 George Street: ashlar with rubble returns.
  - York Street: ashlar rears. Rear treatment varies.
  - Cottages and quarrymen's housing (for example Oxford Place, Combe Down) are coursed rubble, sometimes with an ashlar-faced end house.
- **Render and paint** are mostly later alterations, and BPT pushes for their removal.

### Brick
Brick is historically rare in Bath. It appears mainly in:
- railway and industrial structures [?]
- some post-1990 housing, for example Mulberry Park ("predominantly brick with elements of Bath stone")

The council currently refuses reconstituted stone on new work (refused retrospective appeals, as reported by stonespecialist.com).

### Georgian terraces (c.1720–1830)
| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Storeys | 3 storeys plus attic plus basement for grand terraces. The Royal Crescent is "nearly 50 ft (~15 m)" high, with 3 storeys, attics and basements. Lesser streets have 2–3 storeys. |
| Floor heights | Ground floor about 3.5 m. First floor (the piano nobile) about 4 m, with the tallest windows. Basement 3.2–3.6 m. These come from estate-agent figures and are indicative only. |
| Parapet | A cornice and parapet (sometimes balustraded) hides a double-pitched "M" roof or a mansard with dormers. Mansards have slate on the outer slope (for example Great Stanhope St). |
| Basement | A sunken "area" in front of the house, with cast or wrought iron railings on a stone kerb, a bridge to the front door and steps down. |
| Windows | Timber sashes, 6-over-6 for Georgian. Victorian replacements are 1-over-1 or 2-over-2 with horns. |
| Chimneys | Ashlar stacks on the party walls, with a row of clay pots. One stack per party wall serves both houses. |
| Roof | Welsh slate dominates. Some terraces carry clay double-Roman tiles, often 1930s or later replacements (for example St George's Place, Kingsmead). |

### Victorian and Edwardian housing (1840–1914)
- **Villas:** detached or semi-detached Italianate villas in Bath stone ashlar with low hipped slate roofs and canted bays, set in gardens. Examples: Oldfield Road, Upper Oldfield Park, Wellsway (built around 1897), Bloomfield Road pairs, Newbridge Hill.
- **Terraces:** 2-storey terraces, often with a basement on sloping ground. They have ashlar fronts and two-storey canted bays (for example Newbridge Road, where the bay runs from lower ground to ground floor), slate roofs and small front gardens behind low stone walls.

**Housing types by date:**
- **Inter-war:** 2-storey semis and short terraces with hipped or gabled roofs. Many are rendered or Bath stone [?]. Roofs are clay double-Roman or concrete interlocking tiles [?].
- **Post-war:** 2-storey terraces and semis in **reconstituted Bath stone with concrete tile roofs**. This is documented for Weston's postwar terraces by the 2005 City-wide Character Appraisal. Moorlands (1945) is natural Bath stone, 2-storey.
- **Prefabs** went up in 1946–48 at Odd Down (around Winscombe Road). They have since been demolished [?].
- **Blocks:** low-rise flat blocks of 3–4 storeys, plus some point blocks at Snow Hill and Kingsmead [?].

### Modern housing (1990–present)
- Mixed brick and Bath stone.
- 2–3 storey houses plus 3–5 storey apartment blocks [?].
- Examples: Mulberry Park, Bath Western Riverside [?].

### Roof coverings
| Covering | Where it dominates |
|---|---|
| Welsh slate | Georgian and Victorian city, villas, ashlar terraces, Church Road in Combe Down |
| Clay double-Roman tiles (Bridgwater type, red/orange, 30° min pitch) | Vernacular cottages, rubble terraces, Combe Down quarrymen's cottages (Oxford Place), some replaced Georgian roofs, inter-war houses |
| Pantiles | Mixed in with double-Roman on cottages. Often confused with double-Roman in listings. |
| Concrete interlocking tiles | Post-war council estates and recon-stone terraces, later rear slopes |
| Stone slates | Rare. Rural outskirts [?] |

## 3. Streetscape

### Street names
- **Historic signs** are carved (V-section) into the building stone in high-contrast serif capitals, and some are painted directly onto the wall. Some carry traces of black or dark paint [?].
- A World Heritage repair programme has run since 2010 (52 signs restored by the end of 2018). It re-cuts lost letters and repaints in Keim mineral paint to match the existing colours.
- **Modern plates** are not documented. The national template is black text on a white plate with a black border [?].

### Lighting
- **Historic:** mid-C19 cast-iron former gas lamp posts with lanterns, many of them listed and converted to electricity. North Parade Bridge lanterns were refitted with LEDs in 2026, and Sydney Buildings has replica lanterns.
- **Suburbs:** plain grey or galvanised steel columns [?].

### Wayfinding and furniture (2011 onwards)
- The system was designed by Pearson Lloyd with FW Design.
- **Monoliths:** rectangular bronze monoliths with a circular vitreous-enamel map.
- **Furniture:** matching bronze-and-timber benches, bins, bollards, cycle stands and fountains.
- **Bus shelters:** the council chose green shelters with sloped roofs and bench seats in 2012, for the Bath Transportation Package routes.

### Pavements and roads
- **Pavements:** Pennant sandstone flags (grey-blue, large slabs) have been standard in the centre since around 1727. Side streets historically used Lias. There are raised pavements with railings and Pennant kerbs on London Road, George Street and in Bathampton. Suburbs have asphalt pavements with concrete kerbs [?].
- **Markings:** double yellow lines are ubiquitous. There are many Residents' Parking Zones; zones 22–28 were added around 2023–25:
  - Entry Hill
  - Sion Hill/Summerhill
  - Chelsea/Foxcombe
  - Lyme Gardens/Charmouth
  - Lower Weston
  - Walcot/Snow Hill/Claremont
  - Oldfield Park/Westmoreland (zone 28, extended August 2025)
- Combe Down is not known to be in an RPZ [?].

### Post boxes, phone boxes and shops
- **Post boxes:** standard red cylindrical boxes. Two listed hexagonal "Penfold" boxes stand at Laura Place (1867) and outside 51 Great Pulteney Street (c.1865).
- **Phone boxes:** red K6 boxes survive in places [?].
- **Shopfronts:** painted timber with matte or eggshell finish in deep heritage colours. Fascias are hand-painted rather than vinyl. Timber hanging signs hang on traditional iron brackets, although BPT opposes them on Milsom St.
- **Pubs:** hanging painted board signs on brackets, with lettering on the fascia.

## 4. Combe Down specifics

**Anchor:** the Hadley Arms (51.3624, -2.3452) is set diagonally at the North Road / The Avenue junction. The village's commercial core is **The Avenue** (north–south from North Road towards Church Road) plus the **Bradford Road** parade. Church Road is mainly residential, church and school, and few shops were found there. The post office closed in 2006 and its counter is now inside a grocery on Bradford Road.

Historically, North Road was "pub central" (Hadley Arms, Masons Arms, Horseshoe and Three Crowns within 200 yards). Older Church Road had a butcher, greengrocer, bakery, chemist, Co-op and newsagent (Frank Sumsion's memoir).

### Landmarks
**Holy Trinity Church**, Church Road (51.3591, -2.3456), Grade II
- Built 1832–35 by H.E. Goodridge (architect of Beckford's Tower); aisles and chancel extension 1883–84 by W.J. Willcox.
- **It does have a tower and spire.** The broad west tower base has four square buttresses rising to octagonal shafts with pinnacles. The central two continue up into an **octagonal tower and spire**.
- The chancel is octagonal. A date of 1834 is carved in the door spandrels.
- The gate piers and railings are listed separately (51.3593, -2.3461).
- The Old Vicarage, also by Goodridge, is nearby.

**Union Chapel**, Church Road, Grade II [location approx 51.360, -2.343]
- Opened 1815 as a nonconformist chapel (Baptist, Independent and Wesleyan, hence "Union"), founded from Somerset St Baptist and Argyle St Congregational churches.
- Altered 1880 and 1909; hall range c.1900; schoolroom 1891.
- Burial ground used 1838–1916 (212 burials). Its appearance is not described in the sources [?].

**Quarrymen's housing**
- **De Montalt Place** ("The Old Rank"): Church Road, west of the church [?]. A terrace of 11 quarrymen's cottages attributed to John Wood the Elder, 1729, and the earliest housing on the Down. The centre house has a pediment and sundial ("Dial House"); it was home of Richard Jones, Allen's clerk of works.
- **Isabella Place:** an elegant terrace. Sources date it to the 1770s (Earl De Montalt) or c.1805 [?].
- **Oxford Terrace:** a 2-storey 19C quarrymen's terrace in Bath stone ashlar, a non-designated heritage asset.
- **Oxford Place (1–3):** mid-C19 coursed rubble with some ashlar and **double-Roman or pantile roofs**, 2 storeys, sashes, Grade II.

**Church Road in general:** Bath stone ashlar, slate roofs, hipped profiles, symmetrical chimney stacks. North Road (for example no. 146) has Grade II ashlar terraces with pitched roofs.

**Stone mines and stabilisation**
- Ralph Allen bought the land and quarries from 1726. Mining was underground (the Firs and Byfield mines).
- A 1994 survey found about 80% of the workings had less than 6 m of cover.
- Stabilisation ran about 2004–2009 (largely complete November 2009). About 600,000 m³ of foamed concrete was used (combedown.org gives 620,894 m³: 590,894 foamed plus 30,000 stone) over about 25 ha. Cost about £150m. Between 649 and 760 properties were protected. It was the UK's largest foamed-concrete use.
- **Ralph Allen CornerStone** opened in July 2014 as the legacy and education centre, run by the Combe Down Stone Legacy Trust, with the Heritage Society archive in its basement. The Museum of Bath Stone is also involved.

**Firs Field**, The Avenue, BA2 5EH (approx 51.3605, -2.3440 [?])
- Meadow restored after the mine works in 2010.
- Contains the village war memorial and a play area. Fields in Trust Centenary Field since 2015.
- There is a project to rebuild the remains of a mine shaft wall with a curved seat.

**Ralph Allen's tramway**
- A wooden wagonway carried stone from the Combe Down quarries down the line of **Ralph Allen Drive** (Prior Park Road continues the course) to a wharf at Widcombe on the Avon.
- A 1752 engraving of it is the earliest known railway print. It became a road after Allen's death (1764).

**Prior Park** (mansion approx 51.3650, -2.3444)
- The mansion was designed by John Wood the Elder (commissioned 1728, built c.1733–50) and completed by Richard Jones. It is now Prior Park College; bus stop "Ralph Allen Drive Prior Park College".
- **Palladian Bridge:** 1755 by Richard Jones, Grade I, in the National Trust landscape garden (NT since 1993) on the lakes below the mansion [approx 51.368, -2.344 ?].

**Foxhill estate** (approx 51.3620, -2.3330)
- Council estate, first contract 1950. 542 homes, about 80% owned by Curo.
- Curo planned demolition for redevelopment, dropped it in 2018 after legal action by residents, and is refurbishing instead.
- Form: 2-storey semis and terraces [?]. Materials not documented; likely reconstituted stone or render with concrete tiles [?].

**Mulberry Park** (approx 51.3645, -2.3345)
- Former MoD (Admiralty) Foxhill site, 19 ha, bought by Curo in March 2013. The name comes from the Mulberry Harbours designed in Bath.
- Outline consent September 2014 (up to 700 homes). Phase 1 was 275 homes (approved March 2015, on site spring 2016, first residents spring 2017). Completion expected end of 2026.
- Designed by HTA. About 70% family houses (cottages, town houses, villas, coach houses) with flats in small cluster blocks. 30% affordable.
- **Predominantly brick with Bath stone elements.** Houses 2–3 storeys and apartments 3–4 storeys [?].
- Has a community hub with a café [?].
